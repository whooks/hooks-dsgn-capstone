# How do we know what dinosaurs looked like?

Short answer: Scientists reconstruct dinosaurs from fossils — bones, teeth, skin impressions, footprints, and occasionally preserved pigment — combined with comparisons to living relatives like birds and crocodiles. Some features are known with confidence; others (especially color) are partly informed estimates.

## Bones and skeletons

The foundation is the skeleton. Bone shape reveals body size and proportions, how joints moved, and where muscles attached (muscle scars on bone). By comparing with living animals, paleontologists estimate posture, gait, and likely behavior. When skeletons are incomplete, they infer missing parts from close relatives.

## Skin, scales, and feathers

Rare "skin impressions" and exceptionally preserved fossils show that some dinosaurs had scales, while many theropods (the group leading to birds) had feathers or feather-like structures. Discoveries of feathered dinosaurs, especially from China, transformed how dinosaurs are depicted. Soft-tissue and "mummy" fossils occasionally preserve skin texture in fine detail.

## Color

Color is the hardest trait to recover. In some well-preserved fossils, scientists can find microscopic structures called melanosomes — pigment-containing parts of cells. The shape and arrangement of melanosomes correspond to specific colors in living animals, so their fossilized form can indicate hues like black, reddish-brown, or iridescence. Where melanosomes are not preserved, dinosaur colors in illustrations are educated guesses based on living animals and likely ecology.

## Trace fossils and living relatives

Footprints (trackways) show how dinosaurs stood, how fast they moved, and whether they traveled in groups. Comparing dinosaurs to their closest living relatives — birds and crocodiles, the "extant phylogenetic bracket" — lets scientists infer soft tissues and behaviors that rarely fossilize, such as nesting or air sacs.

## Bottom line

A dinosaur reconstruction blends direct fossil evidence with careful inference. Skeletons and proportions are well supported; skin and feathers are known for many species; precise colors and behaviors are the most uncertain and are revised as new fossils appear.
