# Haolong dongi: a dinosaur preserving never-before-seen skin spikes

Summary: Paleontologists in China described Haolong dongi, an iguanodontian dinosaur whose nearly complete skeleton preserves exceptionally detailed fossilized skin — including hollow cutaneous spikes unlike anything seen before in non-bird dinosaurs.

## What was found

The fossil is a 2.45-meter-long juvenile recovered from the Yixian Formation in northeastern China, dating to about 125 million years ago (Early Cretaceous). Haolong dongi was an iguanodontian — a major group of plant-eating ornithopod dinosaurs that sit evolutionarily between small early bipedal ornithopods and the later duck-billed hadrosaurs.

The specimen preserves overlapping scales on the tail and distinct tuberculate (bumpy) scales on the neck and thorax. Most striking are spikes scattered among the scales. Microscopic imaging showed these spikes are hollow and cylindrical, with a highly cornified (hardened) outer layer over a multi-layered epidermis, keratinocytes preserved down to their nuclei, and a porous dermal pulp at the core.

## Why it matters

This skin anatomy differs from both the protofeathers seen in some other dinosaurs and the scaly spines of modern lizards, suggesting the spikes evolved independently. They probably worked mainly as a deterrent against predators, and possibly aided thermoregulation or sensing. The find gives an unprecedented, cell-level look at non-bird dinosaur skin and shows how complex skin evolution was among ornithischian dinosaurs. Published in Nature Ecology & Evolution.

## Key terms

- Iguanodontian: a group of plant-eating ornithopod dinosaurs.
- Integument: the outer covering of an animal (skin, scales, etc.).
- Keratinocyte: the main cell type in the outer layer of skin.
