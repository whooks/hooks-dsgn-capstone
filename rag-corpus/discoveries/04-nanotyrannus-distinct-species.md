# Nanotyrannus confirmed as its own species, not a young T. rex

Summary: New analysis published in Nature shows that Nanotyrannus was a distinct, fully grown tyrannosaur species — not a juvenile Tyrannosaurus rex, as many scientists had long believed. The team also named a second new species, Nanotyrannus lethaeus.

## What was found

For decades, fossils called Nanotyrannus were widely interpreted as "teenage" T. rex specimens. Researchers James Napoli (Stony Brook University) and Lindsay Zanno (North Carolina State University / NC Museum of Natural Sciences) examined over 200 tyrannosaur fossils, including the famous "Dueling Dinosaurs" specimen from Montana — a Triceratops and a small tyrannosaur preserved together.

Using growth rings in the bones, spinal fusion, and developmental anatomy, they showed the Dueling Dinosaurs tyrannosaur was about 20 years old and physically mature when it died. Its features — longer arms with a vestigial third finger, more teeth, fewer tail vertebrae, and distinct skull nerves — are fixed early in development and are incompatible with growing into a T. rex. They also recognized a second species, naming it Nanotyrannus lethaeus (after the River Lethe, "forgotten," in Greek myth).

## Why it matters

If Nanotyrannus is its own genus, then many past studies that used these fossils to model T. rex growth were actually mixing two different animals. The work suggests predator diversity in the last million years of the Cretaceous was higher than thought, and that other "juvenile" dinosaurs might also be misidentified separate species. The authors describe Nanotyrannus as a medium-sized, long-legged, speedy tyrannosaur that lived alongside T. rex.
