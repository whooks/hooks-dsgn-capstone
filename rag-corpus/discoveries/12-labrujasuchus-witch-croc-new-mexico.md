# Labrujasuchus expectatus: a Triassic "witch croc" that looked like a dinosaur

Summary: Paleontologists described a bizarre, two-legged, toothless Triassic reptile from New Mexico. Despite looking like a dinosaur, Labrujasuchus expectatus belongs to the crocodile branch of the reptile family tree.

## What was found

A team led by the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County named Labrujasuchus expectatus ("witch croc") from fossils at Ghost Ranch, New Mexico. The name comes from "Ranchos de los Brujos," an old Spanish name for the site. It is a shuvosaur — a group of beaked, bipedal (two-legged) archosaurs that lived in the southern United States during the Late Triassic, roughly 235 to 201 million years ago.

Although Labrujasuchus walked on two legs and had a toothless beak like the much later ornithomimosaur dinosaurs, it actually belongs to the lineage of archosaurs that led to crocodiles — animals that are otherwise four-legged, toothy, and semi-aquatic.

## Why it matters

The animal is a striking example of convergent evolution: unrelated lineages independently arriving at similar body plans. In the Triassic, crocodile relatives and early dinosaurs both experimented with bipedal, beaked forms. Labrujasuchus is only the fifth known shuvosaur and conveniently fills a gap between two previously known relatives — hence the species name "expectatus," reflecting that paleontologists expected such an in-between form to exist. Ghost Ranch is also famous for thousands of skeletons of the early dinosaur Coelophysis documented there in 1947.

## Key terms

- Archosaur: the reptile group that includes crocodiles, dinosaurs, and birds.
- Convergent evolution: when unrelated species independently evolve similar features.
