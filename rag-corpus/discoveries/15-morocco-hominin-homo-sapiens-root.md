# 773,000-year-old Moroccan fossils near the root of the Homo sapiens family tree

Summary: Hominin fossils from a cave near Casablanca, Morocco, dated to about 773,000 years old, appear to sit close to the base of the Homo sapiens lineage — strengthening the case that northwest Africa was an important setting for human origins.

## What was found

Researchers led by Jean-Jacques Hublin (Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology) reported new hominin fossils from Grotte à Hominidés at Thomas Quarry, near Casablanca. The remains — two adult jaw fragments, one child's jaw, and several teeth and vertebrae — were collected over three decades and date to roughly 773,000 years ago. The age was pinned down because the surrounding sediments record the Matuyama–Brunhes transition, the last major reversal of Earth's magnetic field, dated worldwide to about 773,000 years ago.

## Why it matters

This is a period genetic studies suggest was critical: when the ancestor of modern Homo sapiens was beginning to diverge from the lineages that became Neanderthals and Denisovans. The Moroccan jaws are surprisingly gracile (lightly built), lack features tying them to European Neanderthals, and keep "primitive" traits linking them to Africa. The researchers interpret them, cautiously, as an isolated population — possibly a late Homo erectus diverging toward Homo sapiens — placing the divergence of human lineages firmly in Africa rather than Europe.

## Caveats

Outside experts describe the team as "appropriately cautious." The fossils may represent a very early Homo sapiens ancestor, but there is not yet enough evidence for a definitive claim. The period from one million to 300,000 years ago is sometimes called the "muddle in the middle" of human evolution, and these fossils alone do not resolve it.

## Key terms

- Hominin: humans and our close extinct relatives.
- Paleomagnetic dating: dating sediments using recorded reversals of Earth's magnetic field.
