# Organic molecules (collagen) found in 66-million-year-old dinosaur bone

Summary: Researchers reported strong evidence that traces of original proteins, including collagen, survive inside a 66-million-year-old Edmontosaurus fossil. This challenges the long-held belief that fossilization destroys all biological material.

## What was found

A team led by the University of Liverpool studied a 22-kilogram Edmontosaurus sacrum (part of the hip) from the Hell Creek Formation in South Dakota. Edmontosaurus was a large duck-billed, plant-eating dinosaur that lived alongside Tyrannosaurus rex at the very end of the Cretaceous Period. Using protein sequencing and several forms of mass spectrometry, the researchers detected remnants of collagen — the main structural protein in bone. Collaborators at UCLA also identified hydroxyproline, an amino acid strongly associated with bone collagen.

## Why it matters

For decades, many scientists assumed dinosaur fossils were fully mineralized rock with no original biology left. Claims of preserved soft tissue — most famously by Mary Schweitzer in a T. rex fossil around 2005 — were often dismissed as modern contamination. By using multiple independent testing methods on the same fossil, this study strengthens the case that the molecules are genuinely original to the dinosaur ("endogenous"), not contamination.

If proteins can persist for tens of millions of years, they could give scientists a new tool to study extinct animals: revealing evolutionary relationships hard to see from bone shape alone, and shedding light on dinosaur growth, physiology, and disease.

## Key terms

- Collagen: the main protein in bone and connective tissue.
- Mass spectrometry: a lab technique that identifies molecules by measuring their mass.
- Endogenous: originating from within the organism itself (not external contamination).

The study, "Evidence for Endogenous Collagen in Edmontosaurus Fossil Bone," was published in Analytical Chemistry.
